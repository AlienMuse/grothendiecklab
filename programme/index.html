Abstract, as published

The Grothendieckian Programme is a programme for machine reasoning about mathematics, set out in seven works of the WiseMotif Monograph of Mathematical Intelligence. This book is their overview, and it adds no mathematical result to them.

Present language models are trained to predict the next token. The seven works do not propose to replace them. They ask what any machine that reasons over local evidence must answer to, and what it would take for a machine to do what Grothendieck did with a hard problem: to build the setting in which the problem becomes natural, and to pose the next question there.

The answer is given as an architecture. At its centre, The Grothendieckian Machine (Series 01) holds mathematical worlds as typed, versioned and verifiable objects, with the status of every claim part of its type. Over it, Topos-Native Intelligence (Series 02) reasons inside a world as inside a topos: a context is a site, the evidence a sheaf, a conclusion a global section that exists only where the local data glue, and a failure to glue an obstruction that can be located. Around them, five research subjects (Series 03–07) — the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation, the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, the Hodge conjecture, the abc conjecture and the Langlands programme — carry the method of recursive mathematical discovery through in full: a structure is proposed, tested against the cases in which the answer is known and abstracted where it survives, and the question is posed again; what fails is kept, as an obstruction, a gap or a test.

The book reads the seven works for what each of them changes — in every case the object rather than the statement — states the principles they share, and sets out the discipline of status labels, ledgers and falsification tests by which they are held. None of the seven works claims a proof of the problems it studies. Each says what it proves, what it reduces to stated hypotheses, and where it stops.

§1.1 The question

A system trained to predict the next token can answer a remarkable range of questions, and in October 2026 an unreleased model was reported to have written hundreds of research manuscripts (Chapter 2). The Grothendieckian Programme is concerned with a different capacity. Grothendieck's way with a hard problem was seldom to attack it where it stood: he built the setting in which the problem became natural, and let the solution come to it (Series 02, Preface). The Programme asks whether a machine can take part in mathematics of that kind — whether it can construct a world in which a problem becomes natural, test that world against everything already known, say precisely where it fails, and pose the next question there — and what such a machine must be, internally, if anything it concludes is to be trusted.

The question is not whether a machine can produce text about a famous problem. The five research subjects of the Programme put it in the same words: it is whether the method can raise a structure around the problem, check that structure against everything already known, and say precisely where it fails (Series 07, Preface).

§1.3 The architecture

Seven works carry the Programme, and Figure 1 draws them as seven cells of one honeycomb (Figure 1). At the centre stands The Grothendieckian Machine (Series 01), which sets out how mathematical worlds are constructed, compared and carried from one setting to another, and how a machine may hold them as typed, versioned and verifiable objects. Above it, on a shared edge, stands Topos-Native Intelligence (Series 02), which states what an intelligence must be, internally, if it is to reason inside such worlds: reasoning native to a topos, in which a conclusion exists only where the local evidence glues. Around the two stand five research subjects — the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation (Series 03), the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (Series 04), the Hodge conjecture (Series 05), the abc conjecture Series 06 and the Langlands programme (Series 07) — on each of which the method is carried through in full, with every step recorded in a form that a mathematician can check and a machine can execute (Series 07, Series Note). §1.4 The seven works in numbers

The seven works run to 34 volumes and 26,021 pages in 995 chapters. They contain more than 349,000 formulas, more than 10,500 numbered theorems, propositions, lemmas, corollaries, definitions and conjectures, 714 algorithms and 1,434 figures and plates. Each count was taken by two independent methods from the sealed editions; where a work publishes its own count, that count is used, and where the two methods differ the smaller is given as a lower bound. Figure 3 sets the counts side by side. The numbers measure extent, not achievement; what the works establish is set out in their own terms in Part II.

§1.5 The boundary

None of the seven works claims a proof of the problems it studies. The Riemann Hypothesis, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, the Hodge conjecture and the abc conjecture remain open, and so does the Langlands correspondence over number fields beyond the cases the literature has proved; each work says so in its first pages, in a sentence of its own. What each offers is, in the phrase the five research subjects share, the state of the question after the method has been applied without concession: what has been proved, what has been reduced to stated hypotheses, and where each route stops (Series 03, Series Note; Series 07, Series Note). Chapter 10 quotes each boundary in the work's own words.

The seven works are numbered 01 to 07 in the WiseMotif Monograph of Mathematical Intelligence. This book is numbered 00 because it stands before them as their overview; it is not an eighth work of the Programme, and everything it says about the seven is taken from their published editions.

This book adds no mathematical result to the seven. It is their overview: Part I sets out the idea of the Programme and its principles, Part II its architecture and the five research subjects, and Part III what the seven works share — the move each of them makes, the discipline that holds them, what they claim and do not claim, and where a reader may enter and contribute.

Source: Series 00, §1.5

§10.1 The boundary of each work, in its own words

Each work of the Programme states its boundary in its first pages. They are quoted here as printed.

  • The Grothendieckian Machine: "The phrase Grothendieckian Machine names a research architecture, not a completed universal solver." (Series 01, Series Note)
  • Topos-Native Intelligence: the work "has several proved local specialisations and no proved global subsumption or replacement result." (Series 02, Abstract)
  • Arithmetic Spectral Descent: "The Riemann Hypothesis remains open. This work does not claim a proof of it, of the generalized Riemann hypotheses, or of any equivalent statement." (Series 03, Series Note)
  • Fractured Adelic Geometry: "The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture remains open. This work does not prove it, proves no case of it that was not already known, and proves the finiteness of Ш for no curve." (Series 04, Series Note)
  • The Archimedean Prism: "The Hodge conjecture remains open. This work does not prove it, and proves no case of it that was not already known." (Series 05, Series Note)
  • Strained Prismatic Geometry: "The abc conjecture remains open. This work does not prove it." (Series 06, Series Note)
  • The Arithmetic Topos: "This work does not prove the Langlands correspondence, and no case of its Main Conjecture is established unconditionally in it." (Series 07, Series Note)

Source: Series 00, §10.1

§10.2 What this book claims

This book claims what an overview can claim, and no more. It reports the seven works with the status each gives its statements; it adds no mathematical result to them; and where it compares them, it compares what they say. The principles of Chapter 3 are stated because each is stated in at least four of the works, not because this book proves them; the moves of Chapter 8 are set side by side because the works make them, not because this book judges them.

It also refrains from three things. It does not raise the status of any statement: a conjecture of one of the works remains a conjecture here, a conditional theorem keeps its premises, and a computation remains a computation. It does not merge the label systems of the seven works beyond the correspondences they themselves state. And it does not judge the work of others. Where it reports a result from outside the seven works, it reports the result as its authors describe it and gives the original source.

The Programme states an aim, and it is stated as an aim: what EGA and SGA did for algebraic geometry — supply the ambient worlds in which problems become natural — the Programme aims to do for machine reasoning about mathematics. Whether it does so will be decided by the work that follows, and by readers who test it.

Three papers on the Programme

Three papers introduce the Programme in article form: an overview, twelve theses and fifty-seven open problems. They were published on Zenodo on 2026-10-28 under CC BY 4.0, each with its own DOI, and the theses and the problems cite the overview. Their repository holds the 57 problems as issues, with the papers' sources, data and figures.

  1. OVERVIEW · 2026-10-28 · 10 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The Grothendieckian Programme: An Overview

    Seven Open Works on Mathematics and Machine Reasoning

    Two of the works specify a reasoning architecture — a core that holds mathematical worlds as typed, versioned and verifiable objects, and a kernel that reasons inside such worlds by sites, sheaves and descent.

    This paper describes the architecture, the five research subjects, the method, the published corpus and the open process by which the works are to be reviewed and continued.

    PDF · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23734421

  2. THESES · 2026-10-28 · 26 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The Rising-Sea Theses

    Twelve Theses on Mathematics and Machine Reasoning

    Each thesis is stated in one sentence, argued, tied to the passages of the seven works on which it rests, related to existing work, and paired with an observation that would refute it.

    The theses concern the unit of mathematical knowledge for a reasoning system, the place of epistemic status, the latent space of mathematical reasoning, attention, failure, authority, and the method of discovery.

    PDF · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23734423

  3. PROBLEMS · 2026-10-28 · 54 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The 57 Grothendieckian Problems

    Open Problems for Mathematics and Machine Reasoning from the Grothendieckian Programme

    Thirty-five come from the two works on the architecture of a reasoning system and twenty-two from the five works that each take a named conjecture or programme as research subject; no problem is one of those conjectures.

    Each problem is stated in the words of its source, located by work, chapter and page, given the status the source assigns it, and paired with a criterion that decides when it is solved, a note on existing work and the date of a literature check.

    PDF · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23734426 · Repository
