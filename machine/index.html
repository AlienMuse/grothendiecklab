SERIES 01 The core: GAI-MUA: The Grothendieckian Machine

§5.1 A verification-first operating system for mathematical worlds

The first work describes itself in one sentence: GAI-MUA is designed as a verification-first operating system for mathematical worlds, not as a monolithic language model (Series 01, Vol. III, Architectural Prospectus). Its organising conviction is that major advances frequently arise not from manipulating isolated formulas more aggressively, but from constructing an ambient world in which the objects, transports, invariants and local-to-global laws of a problem become visible (Series 01, Series Note). Two obligations follow and cannot be separated. The mathematical one is to explain schemes, topoi, descent, cohomology, motives, higher structures and modern arithmetic geometry with exact hypotheses and a source-aware lineage of proof. The architectural one is to express mathematical worlds as typed, versioned artifacts that carry their provenance and can be searched, compared, formalised, computed with and evolved — without allowing statistical confidence to masquerade as proof.

The central principle of the architecture is that a mathematical world, rather than an isolated token sequence, is the unit within which a claim or a computation becomes interpretable (Series 01, Vol. III, Preface). A query is compiled against an admitted world, a target, a closure of assumptions, a supported fragment and a resource envelope; a missing construction produces an obstruction artifact rather than a guess; a proposal for a successor world records its predecessor and what it adds, forgets and assumes. The first work names the shift exactly: computation moves from token continuation to world-scoped action.

Source: Series 00, §5.1

§5.2 Worlds, bridges and obligations

A world in this sense is more exact than a context or a domain. It is a structured environment in which expressions are well formed, objects have declared types, morphisms have variance, locality has a specified meaning, constructions have universes and claims have admissible modes of verification. Its descriptor records the ambient categorical setting, the classes of objects and morphisms, a topology or other locality structure, the logical and universe discipline, the invariants available, the admitted transformations, the standing hypotheses and — the part the first work calls indispensable — the boundary of what is supported (Series 01, Integrated Preface). Two artifacts can share a vocabulary while inhabiting different boundaries, and the descriptor keeps each computation aligned with its actual scope.

A bridge is a directed relation between worlds: a functor, an adjunction, a localisation, a realisation, a change of base, a comparison, a truncation, a completion or a reconstruction procedure. Its record states the direction, what is preserved, what is lost, what return data exist and what compatibilities remain open. Preservation and reflection are recorded separately, and loss is allowed to be productive: a forgetful functor or a truncation discards structure deliberately, and a record that says exactly what was discarded lets later reasoning use the result at its actual strength. Obstructions are the dual unit — a failed lift, a non-zero class, an incompatible overlap, a missing inverse or a countermodel — and each says why an attempted construction remains open (Series 01, Integrated Preface).

The unit of intelligence that follows is the structured inquiry rather than the isolated answer. Choosing the right world, identifying an inadmissible bridge, exposing a missing hypothesis, finding a counterexample to effectivity, or abstaining because the supported fragment is too small are all intelligent actions in this account. The role of a language model is placed accordingly: it can propose world descriptors, locate possible bridges and translate prose into candidate representations, while exact source locators, dependency closures and relation strengths reside in external artifacts (Series 01, Integrated Preface).

Source: Series 00, §5.2

§5.3 Status is part of the type

Under the architecture's contract every claim must carry a world, assumptions, scope, epistemic status, provenance and a closure of its dependencies (Series 01, Vol. III, Architectural Prospectus). The status codes E, S, D, R, C, O and Q — established, source-grounded synthesis, project-native definition, falsifiable research construction, conjecture, obligation and disputed — form a tagged type rather than a confidence ladder, and the same artifact may carry several of them about different aspects (Series 01, Vol. III, Preface). Status governs retrieval and generation in particular. Citation frequency, vector similarity, model confidence, consensus, novelty, benchmark performance, elegance and reward may rank candidates or allocate review; semantic authority arises only from exact evidence and a named decision.

Authority is divided into five planes: mathematical meaning, proposal and discovery, certified computation, evidence and governance, and operations. The planes are not five services but five domains of authority, and the rule that joins them is a chain of non-implications: operational completion does not imply mathematical validity, which does not imply epistemic promotion, which does not imply publication (Series 01, Vol. III, Ch. 3). Each implication needs its own input, judgment and authority. The planes exist, in the first work's words, to prevent a fluent, highly connected proposal system from becoming a truth authority merely because its functions share one orchestration surface. The same separation runs through the proof path: a kernel's acceptance states that particular bytes check against particular target bytes under particular rules, and source fidelity, axiom policy and publication each require their own judgment (Series 01, Vol. III, Preface).

The prospectus of the third volume states the division of labour in four sentences. Retrieval may supply candidate evidence but cannot change truth status. A named, pinned formal kernel may check a proof term against a formal target, while the fidelity of that target and the authority to publish remain separate obligations. Computational systems may supply scoped evidence when their inputs, bounds, environments and replay conditions are recorded. Humans authorise publication (Series 01, Vol. III, Architectural Prospectus). Figure 4 draws these divisions together.

Underneath the constitution lies a memory. The thin waist of the architecture is a typed, immutable, content-addressed fabric of artifacts and events: once a record is sealed, every correction, refinement, retraction or replay produces a new record linked to its predecessor (Series 01, Vol. III, Preface). Failed searches, rejected translations, stale receipts, unresolved obligations and conflicting branches remain addressable, and a later success may supersede an earlier failure for a declared use while preserving the failure and its authority ceiling. Mathematical knowledge is treated as governed history — corrigible, scoped, accountable and resistant to silent change.

Source: Series 00, §5.3

§5.6 Six volumes, and what is not claimed

The work is one monograph in six volumes: Foundations, Modern Synthesis, Architecture, Algorithms, Modular Machine and Self-Evolution (Series 01). The first two reconstruct the Grothendieckian and post-Grothendieckian substrate, from schemes and topoi to perfectoid spaces, prismatic cohomology and condensed mathematics; the third derives the architecture from them; the fourth turns it into algorithm contracts and bounded procedures; the fifth assigns those procedures to a modular platform; and the sixth governs adaptation and self-evolution while mathematical truth remains attached to source, proof and review (Series 01, Integrated Preface). The staging is deliberate. In the third volume the boundary towards evolution is inert and read-only: it exposes a telemetry snapshot as its sole operation, and reward, training, mutation, promotion and rollback all begin under the separate constitution of the sixth volume (Series 01, Vol. III, Architectural Prospectus). Self-evolution enters the system only as evidence-bearing change under an externally governed constitution.

The phrase Grothendieckian Machine names a research architecture, not a completed universal solver (S01, Series Note). The third volume is a normative architecture whose feasibility remains an open programme, its contracts predominantly definitions, research constructions and obligations, and it lists its limits in the same place as its proposals: representation is partial, formal verification is specific to each proposition, conformance is fragmentary, and mathematical discovery remains a research objective (Series 01, Vol. III, Preface).

SERIES 02 The kernel: GM-Omega-Infinity: Topos-Native Intelligence

§6.1 The question of the local

The second work began with a narrow question: what does the word local mean for a language model (Series 02, Preface)? A model reads a context and makes claims that the context is supposed to support. Ask which parts of the context may support a claim, and which collections of such parts determine support on their union, and the standard architecture is silent. It has positions, masks and learned attention weights, and these relate one position to another; what they are not known to provide is a notion of covering. Without one, the sheaf condition — that data agreeing on overlaps glue to data on the whole — constrains nothing, because there are no overlaps on which it can act. Such a model may still behave coherently. It is not obliged to, and the distance between exhibiting a property and being obliged to have it is the subject of the book.

If the deficit is structural, a larger system of the same kind will not remove it, any more than more points will make a set into a space (Series 02, Preface). A different kind of structure is required, and the question becomes which structure, built how, and obliged to what.

Source: Series 00, §6.1

§6.2 Reasoning native to a topos

The answer of the second work is that reasoning should be native to a topos (Series 02, Series Note). A context is treated as a site: its admissible pieces of evidence, the ways in which one piece refines another, and the families of pieces that jointly cover a claim. What the context supports is a sheaf on that site, an assignment of local information that restricts consistently from larger pieces to smaller ones. A conclusion is a global section, and it exists only where the local information glues. Where it does not glue, the failure is not noise; it is a cohomology class — an obstruction with a definite location and a definite size, which a system can compute, report and act upon (Series 02, Preface).

Three consequences organise the architecture. Agreement is a condition to be discharged, so every layer must say whether it preserves restriction and, if it does not, what it measures in its place. Disagreement is information, so the obstruction to gluing is an output of the system rather than an error to be suppressed. Change of context is a morphism, so adaptation, transfer and retrieval are base change along a map of sites, and the question of what survives a change becomes the question of what the map preserves. A fourth consequence is quieter: a topos carries an internal logic in which truth is local, and a reasoning system built in this way returns, with every verdict, the part of the site on which it holds.

Source: Series 00, §6.2

§6.3 The kernel in seven operations

Stripped to its operations, the kernel does seven things (Series 02, Preface), which Figure 5 sets in order. It covers: from a pool of evidence it constructs a site, with covering families that say when a collection of pieces is enough, posed as construction under a stated criterion rather than as the selection of the highest-scoring items. It assigns a sheaf over that site, with stalks whose dimensions may differ from piece to piece. It transports: attention is rebuilt as descent, so that values are carried along restriction maps into the shared stalk of an overlap and compared there. It glues, by a named solver that reports the residual where gluing is impossible. It obstructs, measuring what fails to glue and returning it; a head that lacks independent overlap data abstains rather than inventing it. It decides, evaluating propositions by Kripke–Joyal forcing, so that each verdict comes with the part of the site on which it is forced. And it changes base, expressing transfer to a new context as a morphism of sites that states what it preserves and what it forgets.

On this footing the work builds nine layers over a single base, from the context site to the transfer layer, and a mixture of experts in which each expert is an open sub-topos, with its own local logic and sections, and routing is the construction of a sufficient cover rather than the choice of a fixed number of experts (Series 02, Abstract). Where experts overlap, their sections must agree, and the extent to which they fail to agree is measured by the cohomology of the cover; at the next order, the consistency of their pairwise reconciliations is measured by a gerbe. Experts share no ambient universe, and every comparison between two of them names the link along which it is made.

The mathematics behind the kernel comes from seven lineages — Grothendieck, Deligne, Faltings, Voevodsky, Scholze, Lurie and Mochizuki — and each borrowing is admitted on a single condition: that removing it would break a named definition, theorem or algorithm of the book (Series 02, Series Note). What survives that test is restated without its source vocabulary and turned into algorithms with stated costs, degeneration conditions and refutation gates; what does not survive is recorded, with the reason, among the negative results. Each lineage contributes a different repair to the typing of a reasoning system — a variable base, weights that refuse to infer strictness, an almost-zero that is not zero, coherent descent at all levels — and the repairs interoperate only after three errors are prohibited: a numerical tolerance is not an almost ideal, a finite pairwise consistency check is not higher descent, and equivalence within one typed universe is not permission to identify objects of different species (Series 02, Preface).

A further rule governs every number the kernel reports. A number is worth reporting only if it would not change under choices that should not matter, so every reported quantity carries either a proof that it is invariant under a declared class of transformations or a stated budget for how far it may move under them (Series 02, Preface). The choice of gauge in a stalk, of basepoint for a loop and of cover for a computation are each treated as a first-class ambiguity with its own budget.

Source: Series 00, §6.3

§6.4 Learning under the obligation to glue

An architecture obliged to glue can still be trained out of its obligation, and much of the second work's learning theory is about preventing that (Series 02, Preface). The instructive case is negative. Sheaf neural networks were proposed to learn restriction maps, and they were observed again and again to learn the identity sheaf and become ordinary graph networks. The book shows why: the gluing loss they minimised has a minimiser that does not depend on the data, so that an optimiser can satisfy the loss by destroying the structure the loss was meant to enforce. An objective is therefore admissible only if the structure it rewards cannot be reached by collapsing the site.

Pretraining is read in the same terms as the construction of coverage and descent rather than as the prediction of the next token alone; continual learning becomes base change along a morphism of sites; and in reinforcement learning reward is verified by descent rather than predicted (Series 02, Preface). One identity in this part reaches into current practice: the advantages used by group-relative policy optimisation are the degree-zero Laplacian of the rollout nerve applied to the reward, which shows that subtracting a group mean is one particular choice of cover and opens the choice of others as a design variable.

Source: Series 00, §6.4

§6.5 What is proved, and what is not

Every claim of the second work carries one of four grades — theorem, measurable, design criterion or metaphor — and every unfinished obligation one of four status tokens: open, untrainable, undetermined or withdrawn (Series 02, Abstract). Several results about the architecture's own constructions are proved: the non-abelian capacity of an orthogonal cellular sheaf is either zero or bounded below by a constant that depends only on the stalk dimension; generic weighted attention does not factor through the coend often invoked to explain it; for every k≥2 there is a matched-budget instance on which top-k routing is strictly redundant; and four experts on the boundary of a tetrahedron are the smallest configuration in which second-order disagreement can be non-trivial (Series 02, Preface). Under a declared numerical contract, a parameter injection realises the Transformer's forward map inside the architecture bitwise, with equal gradients and an equal training trajectory. The book adds at once that this equality runs in one direction only: it is an equality of programs, not of architectures, and not a claim of replacement.

The status of the whole is stated once and is not softened anywhere: the work is a research architecture and a structural augmentation for Transformer and mixture-of-experts systems, with several proved local specialisations and no proved global subsumption or replacement result (Series 02, Abstract). It reports no experiment; the experiments it specifies are pre-registered and wait to be run, and each module names the measurement that would force it to be abandoned. Within the Programme it is the part on which the rest must reason (Series 02, Preface).

§4.2 Centre and kernel

The second work describes the arrangement in its own words: the Programme has a centre and a kernel (Series 02, Preface). Its centre is the first work, which described how mathematical worlds are built, compared and transported, and how a machine may hold them as typed, versioned and verifiable objects — worlds with their ambient categories and logics, bridges with their preservation profiles, obligations with their evidence. That work specified the worlds and the traffic between them. It did not specify the reasoning that must take place inside a world when evidence arrives piecemeal, overlaps imperfectly and must be reconciled before anything can be concluded. The second work supplies that kernel.

The two meet at the site. A world of the first work presents its evidence as a site: its admissible pieces of evidence, the order in which they refine one another, and the families that cover a claim. The kernel of the second work places a sheaf of local information over that site and computes what glues. A bridge of the first work becomes a morphism of sites, and the question the first work asks of a bridge — what it preserves — becomes a question the second can answer by computing what the morphism carries across and what it loses; an obligation becomes an open cell of a contract, printed as open until it is discharged (Series 02, Preface). The correspondence is what allows the Programme to be built as one system rather than two: the worlds are specified once, and every reasoning component reasons over them in the same way, under the same discipline of gluing and with the same account of failure. It fixes the order of dependence as well: the kernel needs only that evidence be presented as a site and changes of context as morphisms, and the core needs from the kernel an account of reasoning that respects the boundaries it draws between theorem and design, established and conjectural, a result and its proxy.
