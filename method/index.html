GROTHENDIECK LAB · WISEMOTIF.AI · THE METHOD

Recursive mathematical discovery is recursive self-improvement, held to the standard of proof.

A structure is proposed; it is tested against the cases in which the answer is known; what survives is abstracted into a new setting, and the question is posed again there. What does not survive is not discarded. It becomes a conjecture, a gap or a falsification test, with a statement, a status and a place in a ledger, and it is carried forward as part of the result.

— Series 00, §1.2

§2.3 What Grothendieck did instead

The mathematics the Programme takes as its model worked differently. Grothendieck described the unknown thing to be known as a stretch of hard marl, resisting penetration, and the sea that advances insensibly in silence until it finally surrounds the resistant substance (as quoted in Series 07, Preface). The five research subjects give the same image the form of a contrast: a hard nut can be struck until it breaks, or immersed in a rising sea until it softens and opens of itself (Series 07, Preface).

The Weil conjectures are the example the third work sets out. They were not proved by attacking the zeta function of a variety directly. A cohomology theory was built in which the zeta function became a characteristic polynomial, Frobenius became an operator and the functional equation became duality; the remaining inequality was then a statement about an operator on a finite-dimensional space, and Deligne proved it (Series 03, Preface). The second work names the operations that made such settings possible: schemes, sites and topoi each replaced a space by the system of its local data and the rules by which local data agree; the relative point of view made every construction a construction over a base; the functor of points made an object known by everything that maps into it; and descent made the passage from local to global a matter of data and compatibilities, with obstruction classes measuring how it fails (Series 02, Preface).

What this kind of work produces is not first an answer. It is a world in which the answer can be stated as a property of a well-understood object, and in which the remaining difficulty has a definite location. The first work lists the operations by which such worlds are made — relativisation over a base, representability, localisation and descent, categorical ascent from sets to groupoids and higher structures, derivation, comparison through a larger mediating world, completion and change of characteristic, and reconstruction from shadows — and observes that each has its own hypotheses and its own losses (Series 01, Integrated Preface). A change of world is not an automatic improvement: a localisation may expose local structure while losing global distinctions, and a higher setting may preserve information hidden in an ordinary category while introducing coherence obligations of its own.

Source: Series 00, §2.3

§3.1 The cycle

The Programme has a single research aim: machine intelligence that discovers mathematical structure on its own account. That aim asks for reasoning architectures held to the standard of proof, and for a discipline of recursive mathematical discovery, in which a proposed structure is tested, what survives the test is abstracted into a new setting, and the question is posed again in that setting (Series 07, Series Note). Each of the five research subjects opens with these sentences, and each then records one recursion in full.

The cycle has four steps and one rule (Figure 2). A structure is proposed. It is tested against the cases in which the answer is already known — for the Langlands programme, the function-field case, the de Rham limit, the local fields, the abelian case and the curve of conductor eleven (Series 07, Preface); for the Hodge conjecture, divisors, dimension at most three, cubic fourfolds and abelian surfaces (Series 05, Series Note). What survives is abstracted, and the question is posed again at the new level. The rule is that what does not survive is kept: it becomes a proof obligation or an obstruction, with a statement, a status and an owner, and it is carried forward as part of the result (Series 06, Preface).

The third work shows how the rule changes a programme. Its first route to a spectral realisation of the zeros of the zeta function included a positive-definite polarisation; the test showed that this polarisation is stronger than the Riemann Hypothesis rather than a mechanism from which it follows, and the refutation is the step that identified the index-one formulation the book then develops (Series 03, Preface). The refutation of one polarisation was the step that found the next. Nothing in the procedure makes an argument correct; what it does is make the location of every burden visible.

The test step is designed as carefully as the structure it tests. The third work runs every proposed mechanism on four published counter-models before admitting it, each chosen to expose one source of overreach: a functional equation without an Euler product, an Euler product without the required zero geometry, self-adjointness without the required line, and global symmetry without arithmetic specificity (Series 03, Preface). The fifth derives four design constraints from two negative theorems and runs the principal mechanisms in the literature through them before building anything; most fail before they begin (Series 05, Preface). The fourth tests its axioms over a finite field, where nine of the twelve become theorems, and reports that even there the audit is not uniform (Series 04, Preface). A test that the structure cannot fail does not count as a test.

The output of the cycle is of three kinds that a reader can check, as the fourth work puts it: constructions specified exactly enough to be attempted, conditional theorems proved in full, and obstructions stated precisely enough to be attacked, priced or refuted (Series 04, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §3.1

§3.2 The rising sea as an operation

The rising sea is an image, and the first work turns it into an operation that can be checked. An operational rising-sea event must begin from an explicit obstruction or language deficit, propose a typed extension, compile bounded probes, preserve or explicitly surrender recovery, record costs and residuals, and submit any status request to an authority outside the engines that produced it (Series 01, Vol. III, Ch. 5). Each arrow of that sequence may fail: an obstruction does not prove that enlargement is necessary, a richer vocabulary does not establish that its denotations exist, and a computed bounded result is not an unrestricted theorem.

The method is judged against the original difficulty. An extension that deletes the predicate expressing a contradiction, forgets the invariant detecting failure, weakens equality until disagreement disappears, or renames an unknown as a new primitive has not resolved the original obstruction; it has changed the question (S01, Vol. III, Ch. 5). The first work therefore asks of every change of world an audit: on which objects it is defined, under which hypotheses, what it preserves, reflects, forgets or identifies, how a construction in the new world is shown to exist, what kind of conclusion it can deliver, how one returns to the original question, and what becomes of the counterexamples (Series 01, Integrated Preface). This list, in the first work's words, gives disciplined content to the rising-sea image; and since enlargement also carries a loss profile, valid use makes preservation, loss, scope and return explicit.

Failure, in this account, is an output with a type. The first work treats obstructions, counterexamples, unsupported constructs, non-invertible comparison maps, incoherent overlaps, target mismatches and resource exhaustion as different outputs, because each has a different consequence: a failed attempt may reveal a false theorem, a missing assumption, a category with insufficient expressivity, a topology too coarse, an invariant that does not detect what it should, or a computational fragment that is too small (Series 01, Integrated Preface). The same work names the habit of mind behind the method with a phrase of its own, Diagrammatic Poetry Thinking: the disciplined use of diagrams, universal properties, adjunctions, covers and comparison maps as generators of mathematical questions. A good diagram may reveal a missing arrow or a candidate equivalence; the passage from that insight to a theorem requires hypotheses, source lineage, proof and an exact strength of relation, and a conjecture requires a precise statement and the conditions under which it would be false.

Source: Series 00, §3.2

Recursive self-improvement (RSI)

Recursive mathematical discovery is recursive self-improvement, held to the standard of proof.

What improves, recursively, is the mathematical world and the machine that holds it. No candidate certifies itself, and what fails is kept as evidence for the next generation.

The resulting picture differs from common accounts of recursive self-improvement. Recursion here means immutable succession under stable constraints. Generation n + 1 uses evidence from generation n, including failures and causal analyses, while the record and target of n remain fixed.

— Series 01, Vol. VI, p. 13063

The purpose of Volume VI is to show that meaningful self-improvement and strict epistemic restraint can reinforce one another.

— Series 01, Vol. VI, p. 13075

The success of recursive mathematical discovery, the formalizability of important research judgments, and the reliability of governance remain empirical and institutional questions.

— Series 01, Vol. VI, p. 13076

A self-modifying system can improve its own reported performance by weakening the site, changing the cover, suppressing counter-contexts or making obstruction unobservable.

— Series 02, p. 1484

§3.3 Seven principles

Read side by side, the seven works keep seven principles. Each is stated in at least four of them, in their own words.

Change the world before the problem. When a problem resists, the works do not strike harder at it; they ask what ambient world would make it natural, build that world under explicit axioms, and record what the change gains and loses. Four of the research subjects state the step in one form of words: the recursion changes the object rather than the bridge (Series 04, Series Note), the argument (Series 05, Series Note), the inequality (Series 06, Series Note) or the statement (Series 07, Series Note). The first work calls the same pattern changing ambient worlds (Series 01, Integrated Preface).

A conclusion is a section that glues. A conclusion is licensed only where local evidence agrees on overlaps and glues to a whole. For the second work this is the definition of a conclusion (Series 02, Series Note); for the sixth it is a lesson about mathematics itself — a collection of good local theories is not yet a global theory, and effectivity, comparison and higher coherence are theorems, not punctuation (Series 06, Preface). The third, fourth and seventh works each locate their central difficulty at the passage from local data to a global object (Series 03, Preface; Series 04, Preface; Series 07, Preface).

An obstruction is an output. Where an argument fails, the failure is located, typed and kept. The first work makes failure part of the mathematical state (Series 01, Integrated Preface); the second treats a failure to glue as a cohomology class with a definite location and size (Series 02, Preface); the third keeps a library of scoped obstructions as negative knowledge in usable form, which tells a search where not to go, and why (Series 03, Preface); the fifth records each failure where it occurs, so that the next recursion can begin from it (Series 05, Preface).

No result stronger than its proof. Every claim carries its status where it is made. The five research subjects share the principle in one sentence: no result is presented at a strength greater than its proof (Series 03, Series Note). The first work types status as part of a claim rather than ranking it as confidence (Series 01, Vol. III); the second assigns the grade to the sentence, not to the prose around it (Series 02, Preface); the seventh forbids any upgrade by rhetoric (Series 07, Ch. 40).

Generation is not verification. The producer of a candidate is never its only judge. The first work separates the producer, the independent checker, the proof kernel, the issuer of a receipt, the status authority and the publication authority (Series 01, Vol. III). The sixth gives the reason from experience: fluent generation is not verification, and a model which has just produced an argument is poorly placed to be its only critic (Series 06, Preface). The fourth and fifth put the same discipline into ledgers and adversarial audits (Series 04, Preface; Series 05, Abstract).

Abstraction is earned by what it preserves. A new structure earns its place by what it preserves, the obstruction it exposes and what it makes checkable. The first work says it in five words — abstraction is earned by preservation (Series 01, Vol. III). The second admits imported mathematics only where removing it would leave a named definition, theorem or algorithm ill-typed (Series 02, Preface); the sixth refuses to let a citation transfer established status to a new use (Series 06, Preface); the seventh counts reformulation, elegance and generality as no contribution at all (Series 07, Ch. 40).

The method recurses, and it can fail. Each work states in advance what would refute it. A programme that cannot fail is not a mathematical programme (Series 03, Preface); a framework that cannot fail tells its reader nothing (Series 04, Preface); a framework that cannot be falsified is not mathematics (Series 07, Preface). The second work requires every module to name the measurement whose outcome would force it to be abandoned (Series 02, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §3.3

§8.1 Seven moves

Each of the seven works makes one move that the rest of it elaborates, and Table 2 sets the seven side by side: where the problem stood, what was changed, what the change makes natural, and what it costs. The rows restate the works' own diagnoses and proposals; Chapters 5–7 give the sources.

The table is read row by row and then column by column. Read by rows, it is a list of seven different pieces of mathematics and machine design. Read by columns, it shows a single pattern. In the first column the difficulty is always a missing structure rather than a missing estimate: a language that suppresses an invariant, a context with no covering, a positivity with margins, two quantities with no map between them, a closed current that is not positive, a ring with no base beneath it, a correspondence with no space on which to take a trace. In the second column the answer is always a change of the object in which the problem is posed. The third and fourth columns record what the change buys and what it leaves owing.

§8.2 The common shape

Three features are shared by every row. The first is that the move changes the object and leaves the problem standing. Four research subjects state it in one form of words — the recursion changes the object rather than the bridge, the argument, the inequality or the statement — and the first work names the same pattern as the change of ambient world (Chapter 3). None of the moves weakens the problem it addresses. The fourth work says of its own reformulation that the identity is not easier than the conjecture, and that what the reformulation buys is structure: a single integral statement with visible functoriality, and a decomposition of what remains into pieces that can be attacked separately (Series 04, Preface).

The second is that each move separates local data from the gluing of local data, and finds the difficulty in the gluing. The second work makes this the definition of a conclusion; the research subjects find it in their problems. The local charts of the seventh work's arithmetic curve are established mathematics and their global assembly is conjectural (Series 07, Preface); the local pillars of the sixth work's substrate are established and its global interfaces are open (Series 06, Preface); the fourth work reads every foundational advance of the last two decades as one missing piece of a structure, none of them global and no two of them yet glued (Series 04, Preface).

The third is that each move is tested where the answer is already known, and that the test is allowed to fail. The function-field case is the control for four of the five research subjects — curves over finite fields for the third, fourth and seventh works, the function-field limit of the sixth — and the known cases of the Hodge conjecture are the control for the fifth. In every case the work reports that its specification reproduces the known cases and locates the remaining difficulty in a finite set of named statements: two obligations, O1 and O2, PO-079, the existence of a base (Series 05, Preface; Series 06, Preface; Series 07, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §8.2
