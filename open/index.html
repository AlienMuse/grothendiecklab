Three papers on the Programme

Three papers introduce the Programme in article form: an overview, twelve theses and fifty-seven open problems. They were published on Zenodo on 2026-10-28 under CC BY 4.0, each with its own DOI, and the theses and the problems cite the overview. Their repository holds the 57 problems as issues, with the papers' sources, data and figures.

  1. OVERVIEW · 2026-10-28 · 10 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The Grothendieckian Programme: An Overview

    Seven Open Works on Mathematics and Machine Reasoning

    Two of the works specify a reasoning architecture — a core that holds mathematical worlds as typed, versioned and verifiable objects, and a kernel that reasons inside such worlds by sites, sheaves and descent.

    This paper describes the architecture, the five research subjects, the method, the published corpus and the open process by which the works are to be reviewed and continued.

    PDF · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23734421

  2. THESES · 2026-10-28 · 26 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The Rising-Sea Theses

    Twelve Theses on Mathematics and Machine Reasoning

    Each thesis is stated in one sentence, argued, tied to the passages of the seven works on which it rests, related to existing work, and paired with an observation that would refute it.

    The theses concern the unit of mathematical knowledge for a reasoning system, the place of epistemic status, the latent space of mathematical reasoning, attention, failure, authority, and the method of discovery.

    PDF · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23734423

  3. PROBLEMS · 2026-10-28 · 54 pages · CC BY 4.0

    The 57 Grothendieckian Problems

    Open Problems for Mathematics and Machine Reasoning from the Grothendieckian Programme

    Thirty-five come from the two works on the architecture of a reasoning system and twenty-two from the five works that each take a named conjecture or programme as research subject; no problem is one of those conjectures.

    Each problem is stated in the words of its source, located by work, chapter and page, given the status the source assigns it, and paired with a criterion that decides when it is solved, a note on existing work and the date of a literature check.

The twelve theses

Each thesis is stated in one sentence, argued, tied to the passages of the seven works on which it rests, related to existing work, and paired with an observation that would refute it. (The Rising-Sea Theses, Abstract)

  1. THESIS 1 Worlds, not isolated theorems

    For a reasoning system, the unit of mathematical knowledge is a typed, verifiable world, not an isolated theorem.

    What would refute it A system that stores statements without world records and makes no more transport errors — after base change, change of coefficients or change of variance — than one that stores worlds, on a corpus of the kind specified for Problem 15.

    Problems 4 · 15

  2. THESIS 2 Status is part of the type

    The epistemic status of a statement belongs to its type.

    What would refute it A scalar ordering of statements that preserves, under the composition rules of Series 01, Vol. III, §56.15, every distinction drawn by the seven codes; or a controlled study in which a calibrated confidence score supports the same downstream decisions with no more errors.

    Problems 7 · 8 · 31

  3. THESIS 3 A structured latent space

    Mathematical reasoning needs a structured, non-vectorial latent space.

    What would refute it A vector representation that performs as well as a typed carrier on tasks requiring transport and failure reporting — the counterexample and proof strata of the benchmark of Problem 13 — or a proof that the typed structure is recoverable from the embedding at no loss.

    Problems 6 · 18

  4. THESIS 4 Attention as descent

    Attention can be rebuilt as descent.

    What would refute it The null outcomes of the registered comparisons of Problems 19, 20 and 22; or a proof that transport along restriction maps adds no expressive power over attention in a stated class.

    Problems 19 · 20 · 22 · 24 · 25 · 27 · 28 · 35

  5. THESIS 5 Report failure; abstain without evidence

    A reasoning system should report where local information fails to glue, and abstain when independent overlap data are absent.

    What would refute it A system that never abstains and reports scores only, with fewer errors at equal coverage than one that reports obstructions and abstains; or a proof that the obstruction cannot be computed at useful cost (Problem 21).

    Problems 9 · 16 · 17 · 21 · 23 · 30 · 32 · 47

  6. THESIS 6 A chain of non-implications

    Operational completion does not imply mathematical validity, which does not imply epistemic promotion, which does not imply publication.

    What would refute it A class of problems in which one of the passages is shown to need no separate input — for example a fidelity checker that makes validity of the formal statement imply validity of the intended mathematics (Problem 5).

    Problems 5 · 12 · 26 · 29 · 34

  7. THESIS 7 Machines propose; humans authorize

    Machines may propose; humans authorize publication.

    What would refute it A publication process without human authorisation whose rate of retraction and correction is no higher than that of a human-authorised process on comparable work; or a capability model in which the separation cannot be enforced.

    Problems 10 · 11

  8. THESIS 8 The rising sea

    Hard problems are better approached by raising the surrounding theory than by direct attack.

    What would refute it Comparative evidence that, for a reasoning system at equal cost, direct search settles more problems than theory-building does; or the research subjects of the Programme yielding no result that the extended setting made tractable.

    Problems 1 · 36 · 37 · 38 · 41 · 51 · 52 · 55 · 56

  9. THESIS 9 State what is not established

    A result is not complete until it states what it does not establish.

    What would refute it Evidence that statements of what is not established add nothing that the hypotheses of the positive statement do not already convey — for example, that they can be generated mechanically from the hypotheses — or that readers misuse results no less often without them.

    Problems 33 · 39 · 40 · 43 · 44 · 45 · 48 · 49 · 53 · 54

  10. THESIS 10 Discovery is recursive

    Mathematical discovery is recursive: propose, test against what is known, abstract what survives, ask again.

    What would refute it Machine runs of the recursion that, on problems where the answer is known, rediscover no known result and produce no conjecture that survives its falsifiers (Problem 3); or a body of discoveries in which no abstraction step can be identified.

    Problems 2 · 3

  11. THESIS 11 Creating language

    A mathematical reasoning system must be able to create notation and language, not only use them.

    What would refute it A system restricted to a fixed language that matches a language-creating system on discovery tasks of the kind of Problem 14; or a language-creating system whose new primitives never survive review.

    Problems 14 · 57

  12. THESIS 12 Judge a method by its open ledger

    A method should be judged by an open ledger of what it establishes, what it reduces to stated hypotheses, and what it leaves open.

    What would refute it Ledgers that cannot be made checkable at the scale of a book — Series 06 itself records that the soundness of its ledger is not established — or that readers find less informative than the positive statements.

    Problems 13 · 42 · 46 · 50

§11.1 What is open

Every work of the Programme ends with a list of what it has not done, stated precisely enough to be taken up. The third leaves two construction problems — an arithmetic Hermitian form of negative index one compatible with an operator whose spectrum is the zeros, and an upper constraint on amplified zero data — and invites a reader to attack either without accepting the other (Series 03, Preface). The fourth leaves twelve conjectures and fourteen obstructions, each with a statement, what it blocks, its partial results, an attack plan and a difficulty rating, ordered into a construction programme with criteria for abandoning a stage (Series 04, Preface). The fifth leaves two live obstructions and sixty problems graded by difficulty, and tells a reader who wishes to refute the book, or to complete it, to go directly to the passage from a closed current to a positive one (Series 05, Preface). The sixth leaves ninety-eight named obligations, of which one carries the arithmetic weight of its master inequality (Series 06, Abstract). The seventh leaves four gaps of the first rank of difficulty and fourteen research problems in dependency order (Series 07, Ch. 40). The first two works leave specifications to be implemented and experiments to be run (Series 01, Vol. III; Series 02, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §11.1

§11.2 Three kinds of contribution

The second work names three kinds of contribution that would matter most, and they apply to every work of the Programme (Series 02, Preface). The first is refutation: a counterexample to a theorem, the failure of a stated degeneration, or an experiment in which a module fails the gate it names; each would remove something, and each is welcome. The second is completion: the obligations printed as open are stated precisely enough to be taken up, and several are problems of independent interest. The third is construction: an implementation of a specified part, run against the tests the works fix in advance. The sixth work adds the reason a sceptical reading is not a concession: a reader who finds a false obligation, a circular dependency or a certified failure of a locked comparison has advanced the subject more than one who merely finds the architecture suggestive (Series 06, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §11.2

§11.3 Where to begin

Each work gives its own routes for different readers, and Table 4 assembles from them five routes through the Programme. The sceptical route is the one most of the works recommend: the seventh calls it the recommended route, because its principal claim on a reader's attention is the precision of its accounting, and the accounting is at the back (Series 07, How to Read This Book).

§11.4 Corrections

Each work names the instrument by which it should be corrected. The seventh asks that errors be reported against its statement ledger, which gives every claim a stable identifier, so that a report names the claim it concerns (Series 07, Colophon); the fifth undertakes to keep a public errata (Series 05, Preface); the fourth gives every assertion a row in a proof-obligation ledger, so that a reader may audit the book rather than read it (Series 04, Preface). Each work is published with its sources of record — a concept DOI that resolves to every version, a repository and a signed list of checksums — so that a correction can name the exact edition it corrects.

The Programme is offered in the spirit in which the research subjects offer their own instruments: to be checked, and where it fails, to be corrected (Series 07, Preface).

Source: Series 00, §11.4
